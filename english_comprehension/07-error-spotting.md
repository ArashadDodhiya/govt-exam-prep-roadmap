## STEP 1 — FOUNDATION

**What it is:** A sentence is divided into 3–4 labeled parts (A)(B)(C)(D), with a 5th option (E) usually meaning "No error." You must identify **which single part** contains a grammatical mistake — without needing to state what the correct version would be.

**Why it matters:** A signature SSC question type (very high weightage there), and also appears in banking exams — often 5+ marks.

**Where it appears:**
- SSC CGL/CHSL/MTS — very high weightage, a near-guaranteed question type
- IBPS/SBI, RBI — appears, though sometimes folded into other grammar-testing formats
- Insurance, EPFO, GPSC, Railways — pattern similar to SSC

**How examiners test it:** By hiding a small, single-word error (a wrong preposition, a subject-verb mismatch, a wrong article) inside an otherwise fluent-sounding segment — or, just as often, by including a sentence that has **no error at all**, testing whether you can resist the urge to "find something wrong" when nothing is.

**Key distinction from Sentence Improvement:** This topic tests *recognition* (is this segment right or wrong?), not *production* (what's the correct replacement?) — recognition is generally easier, but the trap is that a single wrong word can hide inside a long, natural-sounding segment.

## STEP 3 — CORE CONCEPTS

- **Definition:** Exactly one of the labeled segments contains a grammatical error (or none do, in a "No error" sentence) — your task is purely to *locate* it, not fix it.
- **Rule:** Only one segment is ever wrong (this is a strict exam convention) — once you've confirmed an error in one segment, you don't need to keep scrutinizing the others as hard.
- **Why it works (as a test):** It isolates pure error-recognition from the harder skill of generating a correct replacement — but compensates by hiding the error more subtly (often in just one word) inside a longer segment.
- **Common mistake:** Reading the sentence for overall *meaning/flow* rather than scanning each segment specifically for grammar — meaning almost always makes sense even when the grammar is broken, so meaning-based reading won't catch the error.
- **Memory trick:** Treat each segment as its own mini grammar-check, independent of whether the sentence "sounds fine" as a whole.

---

## STEP 4 — SEGMENT SCANNING CHECKLIST

Rather than duplicating the full grammar-rule bank (see the Sentence Improvement file's Step 4 for that), use this **scanning order** — check segments in this priority, since it matches where errors are most frequently hidden:

1. **Any segment containing a verb near the subject** → Check Subject-Verb Agreement first. This is the highest-yield check — watch for intervening phrases ("along with," "as well as") and correlative conjunctions ("neither...nor").
2. **Any segment containing a preposition** → Check whether it's the fixed, correct preposition for that verb/adjective/noun (see the collocation tables in the Cloze Test and Sentence Improvement files).
3. **Any segment containing "since" or "for" + a time expression** → Check point-in-time vs duration usage.
4. **Any segment containing an article (a/an/the)** → Check general vs specific reference.
5. **Any segment containing a comparative word** → Check for double comparatives or incomplete "than" comparisons.
6. **Any segment that's a connector/conjunction alone** → Check it expresses the correct logical relationship (contrast/cause/addition) for the sentence.
7. **If all segments pass every check above** → The answer is "No error." Don't force an error onto a segment that's actually fine.

## STEP 5 — SUBTLE SINGLE-WORD ERRORS TO WATCH FOR

| Error Type | Example (wrong) | Corrected |
|---|---|---|
| Singular/plural noun mismatch | "One of the most interesting **book**" | "...**books**" |
| Wrong preposition | "discuss **about** the plan" | "discuss the plan" |
| Missing/extra article | "He is **a** honest man" | "He is **an** honest man" |
| Wrong verb form after "each/every" | "Each of them **have**..." | "...**has**..." |
| Wrong time-preposition | "living here **since** ten years" | "...**for** ten years" |
| Double comparative | "**more** taller" | "taller" |

## STEP 6 — CONFUSION ZONES

| Confused Pattern | What to Check | How Examiners Exploit It |
|---|---|---|
| "The number of" vs "A number of" | "The number of X" → singular verb; "A number of X" → plural verb | One is swapped for the other, breaking agreement |
| Intervening phrase before the verb | The phrase between subject and verb doesn't change the verb's number | "The manager, along with his team, **are**..." (wrong — "is" needed) |
| Correlative conjunction agreement | Verb agrees with the noun **nearer** to it in "either...or"/"neither...nor" | Students match the verb to the *farther* noun instead |
| "No error" vs "subtle error" | A sentence can be fully grammatical even if it reads a little unusually | Don't mistake unusual phrasing for a grammar error — check against actual rules, not "gut feel" |

## STEP 7 — EXAMINER TRAP GUIDE

1. **"No error" trap:** At least some sentences in every set are completely correct — resist the pressure to find *something* wrong just because you're expected to.
2. **Single-word-buried trap:** The error is one small word (a preposition, an article, a verb form) hidden inside an otherwise long, fluent-sounding segment — skimming for "does this sound wrong?" will miss it.
3. **Wrong-segment trap:** Two segments might both feel slightly "off" stylistically, but only one contains an actual rule violation — the other is just unusual phrasing, which is not an error.
4. **Meaning-distraction trap:** A sentence about a complex or technical topic can distract you into reading for content comprehension instead of grammar — stay mechanical.
5. **Last-segment-neglect trap:** Test-takers often rush and under-scrutinize the final segment, assuming errors cluster earlier in the sentence — they don't; check every segment equally.

## STEP 8 — PATTERN RECOGNITION

| If a segment contains... | Prioritize checking... |
|---|---|
| The subject and its verb | SVA — including intervening phrases and correlative conjunctions |
| A preposition | Whether it's the fixed, correct one for that verb/adjective |
| "Since" / "for" + time | Point-in-time vs duration usage |
| A comparative word | Double-comparative or incomplete comparison |
| An article | General vs specific reference correctness |
| Nothing unusual at all, after checking A–D | Consider "No error" seriously — don't force a pick |

## STEP 11 — SPEED TECHNIQUES

- **Use the same scanning order every time** (Step 4) — consistency builds speed far more than re-deciding your approach each question.
- **Check the verb-containing segment first** — it resolves the highest percentage of questions fastest.
- **Once you find one clear violation, stop checking other segments** — only one segment is ever wrong by exam convention, so further scrutiny of the rest wastes time.
- **Don't linger on stylistic unease** — if a segment sounds slightly unusual but you can't name a specific rule it breaks, it's very likely fine; move on.

---

## STEP 12 — MEMORY TECHNIQUES

- **Segment-type drilling:** Practice scanning specifically "verb segments," then specifically "preposition segments," in isolation before combining the full scanning order — this builds faster pattern recognition than solving full questions from day one.
- **Personal single-word-error log:** Keep a running list of the specific single-word errors that fooled you (see Step 5's table) — these patterns repeat.
- **Cross-reference with Sentence Improvement:** Since both topics share the same underlying grammar rules, reinforcing one strengthens the other — review the Sentence Improvement file's Step 4 periodically.

## STEP 14 — ERROR DIAGNOSIS FRAMEWORK

When you get a question wrong, don't just note "wrong answer" — identify *why*:

| Diagnosis | Signs | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Missed a subtle single-word error** | You read for overall meaning/flow and it "sounded fine" | Force yourself to scan each segment mechanically (Step 4), ignoring how natural it sounds |
| **Picked the wrong segment** | You correctly sensed *something* was off but flagged the wrong part | Practice naming the *specific* rule violated, not just a vague feeling |
| **Missed "No error"** | You forced an error onto a segment that was actually fine | Ask: can I name the exact rule this breaks? If not, it's likely correct |
| **Missed an intervening-phrase SVA error** | You matched the verb to the wrong noun | Practice mentally deleting intervening phrases before checking agreement |
| **Rushed the last segment** | You under-checked segment (C) or (D) | Deliberately slow down on the final segment specifically |

## STEP 16 — PREVIOUS-YEAR PATTERN INSIGHTS

- Subject-Verb Agreement and preposition errors remain the two most consistently tested categories across SSC error-spotting sets.
- "No error" as the correct answer appears regularly enough (often 1–2 per set) that it should never be treated as a rare or unlikely outcome.
- Errors are hidden roughly evenly across all segments — there's no reliable pattern of errors clustering in any one position, so equal scrutiny of every segment matters.

## STEP 17 — ONE-PAGE REVISION SHEET

**Before every Error Spotting question, run this mental checklist:**
1. Scan the verb-containing segment first — check SVA, watching for intervening phrases.
2. Scan any preposition-containing segment next — check the fixed collocation.
3. Scan for since/for, articles, and comparatives in the remaining segments.
4. Once you find one clear, nameable violation, stop and lock in your answer.
5. If nothing violates a specific rule after a full scan, confidently pick "No error."
6. Don't let stylistic unease alone drive your answer — name the rule, or move on.

**Trap reminders:** No-error trap • single-word-buried • wrong-segment • meaning-distraction • last-segment-neglect.
