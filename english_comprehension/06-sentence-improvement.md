## STEP 1 — FOUNDATION

**What it is:** A sentence with one part underlined. You're given 4 options — three alternate rewrites of the underlined part, and one "No improvement" option — and must pick whichever makes the sentence grammatically correct (or select "No improvement" if the original is already correct).

**Why it matters:** This is where raw grammar knowledge is tested most directly and heavily — often 5–10 marks across SSC and banking exams combined.

**Where it appears:**
- IBPS/SBI PO & Clerk — very common, often multiple questions per paper
- SSC CGL/CHSL — common, sometimes phrased as "select the most appropriate option to improve the sentence"
- RBI, Insurance, EPFO, GPSC, Railways — pattern similar to SSC/Banking

**How examiners test it:** By offering a "correction" that fixes one grammar issue but introduces a new one, or that's grammatically fine but subtly changes the sentence's original meaning — you must evaluate the *entire* replacement, not just skim for anything that "sounds better."

## STEP 3 — CORE CONCEPTS

- **Definition:** The underlined portion contains exactly one type of grammatical flaw (occasionally none at all); your job is to identify *which* flaw and select the option that fixes it *without* introducing a new one or changing the sentence's meaning.
- **Rule:** A correct replacement must be (a) grammatically valid, (b) consistent with the rest of the sentence, and (c) faithful to the original intended meaning.
- **Why it works (as a test):** It's a direct grammar-application test, disguised as a "which sounds better" question — but "sounds better" is not the actual criterion; rule-compliance is.
- **Common mistake:** Choosing an option because it "sounds smoother," without verifying it actually fixes a real grammatical problem — or over-correcting a sentence that didn't need fixing.
- **Memory trick:** Before comparing options, first diagnose the underlined part yourself — name the specific error you think it has. Then look for the option that fixes *that specific* error, not just whichever option feels most fluent.

## STEP 4 — GRAMMAR RULES REFERENCE (Core Rule Bank)

| Rule Area | What to Check | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject-Verb Agreement (SVA) — basics** | Singular subject → singular verb; plural subject → plural verb. Intervening phrases ("along with," "as well as," "together with") do **not** change the subject's number. | *"The manager, along with his team, **is** going to attend"* (not "are") |
| **SVA — indefinite pronouns** | "Each," "everyone," "everybody," "nobody," "either," "neither" (used alone) are always singular | *"Each of the students **has** submitted"* (not "have") |
| **SVA — correlative conjunctions** | With "either...or" / "neither...nor," the verb agrees with the subject **nearer** to it | *"Neither the teacher nor the students **were** happy"* (agrees with "students") |
| **SVA — "the number of" vs "a number of"** | "The number of X" takes a **singular** verb; "A number of X" takes a **plural** verb | *"The number of applicants **has** increased"* vs *"A number of applicants **have** applied"* |
| **Tense — since vs for** | "Since" + a specific point in time; "for" + a duration | *"...working here **for** five years"* (not "since five years") |
| **Tense — sequence of tense** | If the main clause is in the past, the subordinate clause generally stays in the past too (with some exceptions for universal truths) | *"He said that he **was** tired"* (not "is tired," unless stating a general truth) |
| **Prepositions — transitive verbs need none** | Some verbs never take a preposition before their object (discuss, marry, resemble, reach, request, order, obey) | *"The committee will **discuss** the proposal"* (not "discuss about") |
| **Articles (a/an/the)** | "A/an" for one-of-many/general reference; "the" for something specific/already mentioned/unique | *"**The** sun rises in the east"* (unique object → "the") |
| **Parallelism** | Items in a list, or clauses joined by correlative conjunctions ("not only...but also," "both...and," "either...or"), must share the same grammatical form | *"She likes reading, writing, and **cooking**"* (all gerunds — not "to cook") |
| **Modifier placement** | A modifying phrase (especially one starting a sentence) must sit next to the noun it's actually describing | *"Walking through the park, **Anita** found the flowers beautiful"* (not "the flowers looked beautiful to Anita" — the flowers weren't walking) |
| **Redundancy/wordiness** | Avoid phrases that repeat a meaning already implied by another word in the same phrase | "returned **back**," "repeat **again**," "past **history**," "final **outcome**," "free **gift**," "each and every" |
| **Comparison structures** | Use a single comparative form, not a double one; comparisons need a complete "than" clause | *"She is **smarter** than her brother"* (not "more smarter") |
| **Pronoun agreement/reference** | A pronoun must match its antecedent in number and gender, and its reference must be unambiguous | *"Each employee must submit **his or her** report"* (not "their," in strict formal-exam convention) |
| **Countable/Uncountable nouns** | "Fewer" for countable nouns, "less" for uncountable; "many" for countable, "much" for uncountable | *"**Fewer** people attended"* (not "less people") |
| **Gerund vs Infinitive after certain verbs** | Some verbs take a gerund (enjoy, avoid, suggest + -ing), others take an infinitive (want, decide, plan + to-verb) — memorize these as fixed pairs | *"She **enjoys reading**"* (not "enjoys to read") |

## STEP 6 — CONFUSION ZONES

| Confused Pair | Difference | How Examiners Exploit It |
|---|---|---|
| Since vs For | Since = a specific starting point in time; For = a duration/span of time | "Since five years" is a classic, very frequently tested error |
| Less vs Fewer | Less = uncountable nouns; Fewer = countable nouns | "Less people" vs the correct "fewer people" |
| Who vs Whom | Who = subject of a clause; Whom = object of a clause/preposition | "Whom is calling?" (wrong) vs "Who is calling?" (correct, subject) |
| Each Other vs One Another | Each other = between exactly two; One another = among more than two (strict formal convention) | Sentences involving groups vs pairs swap these |
| As vs Like | "As" introduces a clause (with a verb); "Like" introduces a noun/noun phrase for comparison | *"He behaved **as** his father had taught him"* vs *"He behaved **like** his father"* |
| Different From vs Different Than | Formal/exam convention prefers "different from" | "Different than" is common in casual American English but usually marked wrong in Indian exams |

## STEP 7 — EXAMINER TRAP GUIDE

1. **"No improvement" trap:** Some sentences are already grammatically correct — the trap is feeling pressured to pick *some* correction anyway. Confirm there's an actual, nameable error before rejecting "No improvement."
2. **Over-correction trap:** Changing a part of the sentence that "sounds a bit awkward" stylistically but isn't actually grammatically wrong.
3. **Meaning-change trap:** An option is grammatically valid on its own but subtly shifts the sentence's original intended meaning — always cross-check meaning, not just grammar.
4. **New-error-introduced trap:** An option fixes the original error but introduces a *different* grammatical mistake — you must verify the entire replacement, not just the part that addresses the known issue.
5. **Intervening-phrase SVA trap:** A phrase like "along with," "as well as," "together with," or "in addition to" sits between the subject and verb, tempting you to match the verb to the wrong (nearer but grammatically irrelevant) noun.
6. **Redundancy-missed trap:** A wordy phrase "sounds fine" because it's common in casual speech, even though it's grammatically redundant in formal written English.

## STEP 8 — PATTERN RECOGNITION

| If the underlined part contains... | Check for... |
|---|---|
| A verb close to the subject | Subject-Verb Agreement — especially watch for intervening phrases |
| "Since" or "for" + a time expression | Whether the correct time-preposition is used for a point vs a duration |
| A comparative word (-er, more, most) | Double-comparative errors or incomplete "than" comparisons |
| A list, or "not only...but also" / "both...and" | Parallelism — do all items/clauses share the same grammatical form? |
| A participial phrase at the start of the sentence | Modifier placement — does it logically attach to the correct noun? |
| A phrase like "return back," "repeat again," "free gift" | Redundancy |
| A pronoun (he/she/they/their) | Agreement in number/gender with its antecedent, and clarity of reference |

## STEP 11 — SPEED TECHNIQUES

- **Diagnose before comparing options:** Name the specific error type in the underlined part *first* — this turns a 4-way comparison into a targeted search for the one option that fixes exactly that issue.
- **Read the full sentence with each candidate substituted in:** Don't evaluate an option in isolation — plug it into the sentence and read the whole thing before deciding.
- **Eliminate options that introduce new errors immediately**, even if they fix the original issue — a valid fix must be clean everywhere, not just at the original error site.
- **Don't rule out "No improvement" reflexively** — it's correct more often than test-takers expect, especially in higher-difficulty (RBI/UPSC) papers.

---

## STEP 12 — MEMORY TECHNIQUES

- **Rule-tagging practice:** Every time you solve a question, explicitly name which Step 4 rule it tested — this builds a mental index you can search quickly under time pressure.
- **Build a personal "classic error" list:** Keep adding the specific error patterns you personally get wrong (not just the general rule) — personal mistake patterns repeat more than you'd expect.
- **Redundancy phrase drilling:** Read Step 5's list aloud periodically — redundant phrases are common in casual speech, so your ear needs deliberate retraining to flag them.

## STEP 14 — ERROR DIAGNOSIS FRAMEWORK

When you get a question wrong, don't just note "wrong answer" — identify *why*:

| Diagnosis | Signs | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Rule gap** | You didn't know the underlying grammar rule | Add it to Step 4's table with a personal example |
| **Missed intervening phrase** | You matched SVA to the wrong noun because of a phrase between subject and verb | Practice mentally "deleting" intervening phrases before checking agreement |
| **Over-corrected a correct sentence** | You picked a change when "No improvement" was correct | Re-run Step 7's trap checklist — name the *specific* error before rejecting D |
| **Picked an option with a new error** | You fixed the obvious issue but missed a new one introduced by your choice | Always re-read the *entire* sentence with your chosen option substituted in |
| **Missed a meaning shift** | Your chosen option was grammatically fine but changed the sentence's original meaning | Compare meaning, not just grammar, before finalizing |
| **Didn't recognize redundancy** | The phrase "sounded normal" from casual speech habits | Drill Step 5's redundancy list specifically |

## STEP 17 — ONE-PAGE REVISION SHEET

**Before every Sentence Improvement question, run this mental checklist:**
1. Name the specific error in the underlined part *before* looking at the options.
2. Check for intervening phrases that might be disguising the true subject.
3. Substitute each candidate option into the full sentence and re-read it whole.
4. Eliminate any option that fixes the original error but introduces a new one.
5. Confirm the chosen option preserves the original meaning.
6. Don't avoid "No improvement" out of instinct — verify there's a real, nameable error first.

**Trap reminders:** No-improvement trap • over-correction • meaning-change • new-error-introduced • intervening-phrase SVA • redundancy-missed.

## ANSWER KEY & EXPLANATIONS
*(Only look after attempting all 10 questions in Step 13)*

**Q1: A) has submitted** — "Each" is always singular, regardless of the plural noun ("students") that follows it.

**Q2: A) for five years** — "Since" needs a specific starting point (e.g., "since 2019"); "for" is used for a duration.

**Q3: D) No improvement** — "Players" is plural, so "who have ever played" is already correct — this is a genuine "no error" sentence.

**Q4: A) is going to attend** — The subject is "the manager" (singular); "along with his team" is an intervening phrase that doesn't change the verb's number.

**Q5: A) were happy** — With "neither...nor," the verb agrees with the nearer subject, "students" (plural).

**Q6: A) smarter** — Double comparative error; "more" and "-er" should never be used together.

**Q7: A) discuss** — "Discuss" is a transitive verb and never takes "about" before its object.

**Q8: A) returned** — "Returned back" is redundant; "return" already implies "back."

**Q9: A) Anita found the flowers beautiful** — The original has a dangling modifier: "walking through the park" must describe a person (Anita), not "the flowers," which cannot walk.

**Q10: A) books** — "One of the most interesting ___" requires a plural noun ("books"), since it refers to one item among a plural group.
