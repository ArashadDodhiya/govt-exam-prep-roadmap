## STEP 1 — FOUNDATION

**What it is:** You're shown a set of words (usually 4–5) and asked to identify either the one **correctly spelled** word or the one **incorrectly spelled** word among them. Sometimes it's embedded inside a sentence instead of a standalone word list.

**Why it matters:** A small but easy-marks section if trained properly — usually 2–5 marks, but with near-100% accuracy achievable once the common trap words are memorized, since the pool of frequently-tested words repeats heavily year after year.

**Where it appears:**
- SSC CGL/CHSL/MTS — standalone "spot the correctly/incorrectly spelled word" questions
- IBPS/SBI — sometimes folded into Error Spotting or Sentence Improvement rather than asked directly
- RBI, Insurance, EPFO, GPSC, Railways — pattern similar to SSC

**How examiners test it:** Not obscure or rare words — they overwhelmingly reuse a known pool of "commonly misspelled in everyday writing" words (accommodate, necessary, separate, embarrass, etc.), because these are the words people genuinely get wrong. Memorizing that pool solves most of this topic.

## STEP 3 — CORE CONCEPTS

### 3.1 Doubled-Consonant Errors
- **Definition:** Words where a consonant should (or shouldn't) be doubled, often when adding a suffix.
- **Rule:** When a short word ends in a single vowel + single consonant *and the stress falls on that last syllable*, double the consonant before adding a vowel-starting suffix (occur → occurred; begin → beginning).
- **Exception:** If the stress shifts away from that syllable once the suffix is added, doubling does **not** happen (prefer → preferred **but** prefer → preference, no double r, because stress shifts back to the first syllable).
- **Common mistake:** Assuming all "___-ence/-ance" words double their consonant just because the root word does when taking "-ed/-ing."
- **Memory trick:** Say the suffixed word aloud — if the stress lands right before the suffix, expect doubling; if the stress moves away, expect no doubling.

### 3.2 Silent-Letter Errors
- **Definition:** Words containing a letter that isn't pronounced, which people often drop in writing.
- **Examples:** su**b**tle, dou**b**t, **p**sychology, re**c**eipt, **k**now, **g**host, fo**r**eign (silent-ish depending on accent).
- **Common mistake:** Spelling the word exactly as it sounds, dropping the silent letter entirely.
- **Memory trick:** Learn silent letters as part of the word's "shape," not its sound — visual memory beats phonetic memory here.

### 3.3 IE vs EI
- **Rule of thumb:** "I before E, except after C" — but this mainly applies to words making a long "ee" sound (rec**ei**ve, dec**ei**ve, ce**i**ling — ei after c; bel**ie**ve, fr**ie**nd, ach**ie**ve — ie elsewhere).
- **Exceptions to memorize directly (don't force the rule onto them):** we**i**rd, se**i**ze, ne**i**ther, fore**ig**n, sc**ie**nce, suffic**ie**nt, prote**i**n.
- **Common mistake:** Applying the rule to every ie/ei word, including the ones that are simply exceptions.

### 3.4 Suffix-Attachment Errors
- **Definition:** Errors introduced specifically when a prefix or suffix is added to a root word.
- **Rule:** A prefix ending in the same letter the root starts with usually creates a double letter (dis + satisfied = di**ss**atisfied; un + necessary = u**nn**ecessary; mis + spell = mi**ss**pell).
- **Common mistake:** Dropping the doubled letter because it "looks odd" — these doubled letters are actually correct and expected.

## STEP 4 — SPELLING RULES REFERENCE

| Rule Area | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **-able vs -ible** | "-able" usually attaches to a root that's a complete standalone word; "-ible" usually attaches to a root that isn't standalone (not a perfect rule, but a useful first guess) | comfort**able**, accept**able** vs vis**ible**, ed**ible** |
| **Plurals: -y → -ies** | Change y to ies if preceded by a consonant; just add -s if preceded by a vowel | compan**y** → compan**ies**, but d**ay** → d**ays** |
| **Plurals: -f/-fe → -ves** | Many (not all) words ending in f/fe change to -ves in plural | kni**fe** → kni**ves**, but ro**of** → ro**ofs** (exception) |
| **Prefix + root = double letter** | Prefix's last letter + root's first letter, if identical, stay doubled | dis+satisfied → dissatisfied |
| **British convention (used in most Indian competitive exams)** | -our (not -or), -ise (not -ize, though both are often accepted), -re (not -er), double L before suffix | col**our**, organ**ise**, cent**re**, trave**lling** |

## STEP 6 — CONFUSION ZONES

| Confused Pair | Difference | How Examiners Exploit It |
|---|---|---|
| Stationary vs Stationery | Stationary = not moving; Stationery = paper/office supplies | Both correctly spelled individually — question is about *usage*, not just spelling, so read the sentence carefully |
| Principal vs Principle | Principal = head of institution / main; Principle = a rule or belief | Same trap style — both are "correct" words, context decides which fits |
| Complement vs Compliment | Complement = something that completes; Compliment = praise | Near-identical spelling, opposite-ish meanings |
| Conscience vs Conscious | Conscience = moral sense (noun); Conscious = awake/aware (adjective) | Frequently swapped even by careful writers |
| Desert vs Dessert | Desert = dry land / to abandon; Dessert = sweet course after a meal | Single vs double "s" changes the whole word |
| Colour vs Color, Organise vs Organize | British vs American spelling — both technically "correct" in their own convention | Indian exams generally expect British spelling; watch for passages that mix both inconsistently |

## STEP 7 — EXAMINER TRAP GUIDE

1. **One-letter-off trap:** The wrong option differs from the correct spelling by exactly one letter (single vs double consonant) — the hardest trap to catch by "eyeballing."
2. **Silent-letter-dropped trap:** The wrong option spells the word exactly as it sounds, omitting a silent letter.
3. **Over-doubling trap:** A word that "feels like" it should double a consonant (by analogy with a similar word) but actually shouldn't (e.g., "harass" often wrongly doubled to "harrass" by analogy with "embarrass").
4. **IE/EI reversal trap:** Swapping the two letters in a way that still "looks like a real word."
5. **British/American mixing trap:** A passage otherwise in British spelling suddenly uses an American variant (or vice versa) as the "incorrect" option.
6. **Look-alike-word trap:** A completely different, correctly spelled word is substituted for the intended one (e.g., "conscious" swapped in for "conscience") — this tests vocabulary as much as spelling.
7. **Suffix-attachment trap:** Errors introduced only when a suffix is added to an otherwise-familiar root word.

## STEP 8 — PATTERN RECOGNITION

| If the word ends in / contains... | Check for... |
|---|---|
| -able / -ible | Whether the root is a standalone word (favors -able) or not (favors -ible) |
| A doubled consonant before a suffix | Whether stress falls on that syllable *after* the suffix is added — if not, doubling may be wrong |
| ie / ei making an "ee" sound | Apply "i before e except after c" — but check the exception list first |
| A prefix (dis-, un-, mis-, il-, ir-) | Whether the prefix's last letter matches the root's first letter — if so, expect a doubled letter |
| A French/Latin-origin-sounding word (questionnaire, entrepreneur, bureaucracy) | These rarely follow English patterns — treat as pure memorization |
| -our/-or, -ise/-ize, -re/-er | British vs American convention — default to British unless the passage signals otherwise |

## STEP 11 — SPEED TECHNIQUES

- **Check the ending first:** Most traps live in suffixes, not word beginnings — scan the last 3–4 letters of each option before anything else.
- **Compare options pairwise:** When two options look almost identical, find the exact letter(s) where they differ — that's almost always where the error is.
- **Use the "shape" test:** Read the word as a whole visual chunk rather than sounding it out letter by letter — silent-letter and doubling errors are easier to catch visually than phonetically.
- **Don't trust "familiarity":** A misspelled word can still look familiar simply because you've *seen the wrong version* many times in casual writing — familiarity is not proof of correctness.

---

## STEP 12 — MEMORY TECHNIQUES

- **Mnemonics for the highest-frequency words** (see Step 5's table — use these directly).
- **Group by trap type, not alphabetically:** Learning all "doubled-letter trap" words together (accommodate, embarrass, necessary, occurrence, millennium) builds pattern recognition faster than a random alphabetical list.
- **Write the word, don't just read it:** For your top 20 trouble words, physically write each one 2–3 times — spelling is a motor-memory skill as much as a visual one.

## STEP 14 — ERROR DIAGNOSIS FRAMEWORK

When you get a question wrong, don't just note "wrong answer" — identify *why*:

| Diagnosis | Signs | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Doubling-rule gap** | You missed a single/double consonant error | Re-check Step 3.1's stress-based rule and Step 5's doubling-trap words |
| **Silent-letter gap** | You spelled the word exactly as it sounds | Add the word to a personal "silent letter" list and write it out a few times |
| **IE/EI confusion** | You applied the rule to an exception, or vice versa | Memorize the exception list in Step 3.3 separately from the general rule |
| **Over-doubling error** | You doubled a letter that shouldn't be doubled (by false analogy) | Add the word to Step 5 specifically flagged as an "over-doubling trap" |
| **Look-alike word confusion** | You picked a different, correctly spelled word instead of the intended one | This is a vocabulary gap, not a spelling gap — review Step 6's confusion pairs |
| **Rushed visual scan** | You didn't compare options letter-by-letter where they differed | Slow down specifically on the ending 3–4 letters of each option |

## STEP 17 — ONE-PAGE REVISION SHEET

**Before every spelling question, run this mental checklist:**
1. Identify what's asked — correctly spelled, or incorrectly spelled?
2. Scan the last 3–4 letters of each option first — most traps live in suffixes.
3. For any doubled-letter word, mentally check: does the stress fall right before the suffix?
4. For ie/ei words, check the exception list before applying the general rule.
5. Compare visually near-identical options letter-by-letter to spot the exact difference.
6. Watch for look-alike-word traps (a different, correctly spelled word swapped in).
7. Default to British spelling convention unless the exam/passage signals otherwise.

**Trap reminders:** one-letter-off • silent-letter-dropped • over-doubling • IE/EI reversal • British/American mixing • look-alike-word • suffix-attachment.
