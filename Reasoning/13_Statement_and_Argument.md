# 13. Statement and Argument

## STEP 1: Explaining the Topic from Zero
**What is an Argument?**
In this topic, you are given a Statement in the form of a question representing a policy, a decision, or a social issue (e.g., "Should plastic bags be banned?"). 
This is followed by two Arguments—usually one arguing "Yes" and the other arguing "No". Your job is NOT to decide if the answer should be Yes or No. Your job is to evaluate which argument is **Strong** and which is **Weak** based purely on logic.

**Why do examiners test this?**
It tests your critical thinking and objectivity. Can you recognize a logically sound point even if it goes against your personal beliefs? This is a crucial skill for civil servants, bank officers, and managers. It appears heavily in UPSC CSAT, Bank Mains, and SSC Tier-2.

**How Examiners Create Traps:**
1. **The "True but Weak" Trap:** Statement: "Should homework be banned?" Argument: "Yes, because children love playing video games." *Trap!* It might be true that children love video games, but that is a highly superficial, weak reason to ban homework. A strong argument must have depth.
2. **The Opinion Trap:** Argument: "Yes, I think it is a great idea." *Trap!* Personal opinions are always Weak arguments.
3. **The Copycat Trap:** Argument: "Yes, because the USA has done it." *Trap!* Just because another country or person did it does not make it logically correct for us. This is the fallacy of imitation.

## STEP 2: Subtopics and Variations

### 1. Economic / Administrative Issues
*   "Should taxes be increased?" / "Should the retirement age be lowered?"

### 2. Social / Moral Issues
*   "Should capital punishment be abolished?" / "Should uniform civil code be implemented?"

### 3. Technological / Educational Issues
*   "Should AI be banned in schools?" / "Should online exams replace paper exams?"

## STEP 3: Solving Framework
**How should you think?**
You are a High Court Judge. A lawyer is presenting an argument to you. You must decide if their logic holds up in court.
For an argument to be **STRONG**, it must be:
1. **Relevant:** Directly related to the core of the statement.
2. **Deep (Substantial):** It must touch upon a major social, economic, or legal consequence.
3. **Objective:** Based on facts, universal truths, or established logic, not emotions.

For an argument to be **WEAK**, it usually falls into one of these categories:
1. Ambiguous/Vague
2. Based on a single example or imitation
3. Purely an opinion
4. Superfluous (missing the main point)

## STEP 4: Solving Methods
### Method 1: Traditional (Agree/Disagree)
*   You read the argument and decide if you personally agree with it.
*   *When to use:* NEVER. This leads to massive negative marking.

### Method 2: Fast Method (The "WEAK" Elimination)
*   Instead of looking for the Strong argument, hunt for the Weak ones using the trap criteria (Opinions, Imitations, Superficial reasons). Eliminate them instantly.

### Method 3: Expert Method (The "Because" Test)
*   Read the Statement, then read the Argument using the word "Because".
*   *Example:* "Should X be banned? YES, BECAUSE [Argument]." Does that sentence form a profound, undeniable logical truth? If yes, it's Strong.

## STEP 5: Pattern Recognition Guide (5-10 Second Rule)
*   **Does the argument compare India to another country?** -> Instantly WEAK. (What works there might not work here).
*   **Does the argument use words like "Only", "All", "Never"?** -> Usually WEAK. Extreme logic is rarely sound.
*   **Does the argument state a fundamental Constitutional right or universal scientific fact?** -> Instantly STRONG.

## STEP 6: Shortcuts & Memory Tricks
1. **The Imitation Rule:** "Yes, developed countries do it" = WEAK.
2. **The "Only Way" Rule:** "Yes, this is the *only* way to solve the problem" = WEAK. (There are always multiple ways).
3. **The Past Precedent Rule:** "No, because we have never done it this way before" = WEAK. (Just because it's new doesn't mean it's bad).
4. **The Established Fact Rule:** "Yes, because science proves X causes cancer" = STRONG.

## STEP 7: Common Mistakes
*   **Judging the Conclusion instead of the Premise:** You might believe plastic shouldn't be banned. So if an argument says "Yes, it should be banned because it destroys marine life," you mark it Weak because you disagree with the "Yes." *Mistake!* You must evaluate the *reason* ("destroys marine life"), which is a profoundly Strong environmental fact.
*   **Assuming both can't be Strong:** Sometimes the "Yes" argument gives a strong environmental reason, and the "No" argument gives a strong economic economic reason. **Both I and II can be strong simultaneously.**

## STEP 8: Difficulty Progression

**Level 1 (Very Easy - SSC MTS / GD)**
*   Statement: Should all schools be closed permanently?
*   Arguments: I. Yes, children don't like studying. II. No, education is necessary for a nation's future.
    *   *(I is extremely Weak. II is a fundamental truth, so Strong).*

**Level 3 (Moderate - SSC CGL / CHSL)**
*   Statement: Should the minimum voting age be reduced to 16?
*   Arguments: I. Yes, it will make teenagers more responsible. II. No, 16-year-olds lack the maturity to understand complex political issues.
    *   *(I is Weak/Opinionated. II is Strong as it addresses the psychological requirement of voting).*

**Level 5 (IBPS PO Prelims / State PSC)**
*   Statement: Should the manufacturing of alcohol be banned completely?
*   Arguments: I. Yes, it ruins health and families. II. No, it generates massive tax revenue for the government.
    *   *(BOTH are Strong. I is a strong social reason. II is a strong economic reason).*

**Level 8 (SBI PO Mains / RBI Grade B)**
*   Statement: Should the government privatize all public sector banks?
*   Arguments: I. Yes, it will increase efficiency and reduce NPAs like in Western countries. II. No, private banks prioritize profit over rural financial inclusion.
    *   *(I is Weak because it relies on the "imitation" of Western countries. II is Strong as it highlights a constitutional/social duty of banks).*

**Level 10 (Exam Simulation - UPSC CSAT)**
*   Complex philosophical statements regarding freedom of speech versus state security. Arguments will be densely worded paragraphs. You must pierce the vocabulary and find if the core logic relies on an assumption, an extreme comparison, or a fundamental constitutional truth.

## STEP 9: Practice Session Guidelines
1. **Detach Yourself:** When practicing, if the topic is politics or religion, physically imagine yourself stepping out of your body. You are a robot evaluating code. Is the logic valid?
2. **Analyze the 'Weak' ones:** When reviewing answers, don't just see why the Strong one is strong. Understand exactly which fallacy made the Weak one weak (Imitation, Opinion, Extreme).

## STEP 10: Speed Training
*   **Basic Social Issues:** Ideal: 25s | Expert: 15s (Scan for imitation/extreme traps).
*   **Economic/Policy Issues:** Ideal: 40s | Expert: 25s.
*   **UPSC Paragraph Arguments:** Ideal: 1.5 mins | Expert: 1 min.

## STEP 11: Pattern Mastery Test (Self-Assessment Checklist)
Can you avoid the traps?
1. `Argument: "Yes, because China implemented this 10 years ago."` -> *(Answer: WEAK. Imitation).*
2. `Argument: "No, this is the only way to save the economy."` -> *(Answer: WEAK. Extreme word "only").*
3. `Argument: "Yes, it violates Article 21 of the Constitution."` -> *(Answer: STRONG. Established legal fact).*

## STEP 12: Revision (Cheat Sheet)
*   **STRONG Arguments:** Based on universal truths, scientific facts, constitutional rights, profound economic/social impacts.
*   **WEAK Arguments:** Based on personal opinions, imitation of others, extreme words (Only, Always), superficial reasons, or saying "we have always done it this way."
*   **Remember:** You are judging the *Reasoning*, not the *Conclusion* (Yes/No). Both Yes and No can have Strong arguments.
